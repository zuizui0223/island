# Biogeographic context reshapes the direction and hierarchical expression of island floral assembly

## Full working manuscript v8 — submission-order draft — 2026-09-14

### Abstract

Island floras are often expected to converge toward a coherent floral “island syndrome”, combining greater reproductive assurance with reduced floral specialization or attraction. Yet isolation first filters regional source pools, and the same geographic barrier need not reorganize the same floral components or act at the same taxonomic depth in different biogeographic settings. We therefore asked not only whether floral and reproductive responses accompany island isolation, but whether their **direction, component composition and hierarchical expression** are shared across contexts.

We analysed a fixed universe of 8,265 islands and 106,295 accepted angiosperm species under a progressive analysis contract that fixed hypotheses, support gates, source-pool safeguards, model order and claim ceilings while trait evidence improved. The final snapshot resolved 222,688 of 318,885 possible species-by-axis cells (69.83%) across flower colour, floral structural complexity and reproductive assurance. Mainland distance was treated as a composite gradient of separation, connectivity and source accessibility rather than as a mechanistically pure causal treatment.

A universal floral/reproductive island syndrome was not recovered. In the Palearctic, increasing separation was associated with greater accessibility/generalization and greater reproductive assurance. In all-analysis all-native assemblages, slopes were 0.0795 and 0.0534 per unit of log-distance, respectively (both FDR q=0.00463); direct-only estimates were 0.0616 (q=0.0389) and 0.0966 (q=8.48×10^-14). Tropical direct-only assemblages followed a different trajectory: accessibility/generalization declined with distance (-0.1005, q=0.00275) while reproductive assurance increased (0.1360, q=0.00428). Descriptively, the corresponding eight-component colour–structure–reproduction response fingerprints were approximately orthogonal to moderately opposed between northern-midlatitude and Tropical contexts (95.3–116.5°), indicating differences in component composition rather than the amplitude of one fixed syndrome alone.

The strongest Palearctic response was concentrated at the family-to-genus assembly transition. Family adjustment reduced the magnitude of the primary two-axis distance-response vector by 19.6–33.4%, whereas source-matched genus adjustment reduced the original vector by 78.8–85.9%; genus adjustment therefore removed 70.6–79.1% of the family-adjusted remainder. The primary vector no longer passed its predeclared post-genus gate. A matched secondary audit further showed that genus adjustment altered the same plant-architecture response differently between northern-midlatitude and Tropical contexts across all four source definitions in native non-endemics in both evidence scopes, although support weakened when island endemics were included.

Several intuitive explanations did not earn promotion. Apparent area amplification failed the heteroskedastic-null mechanism gate in all 16 primary classifications; a separately calibrated response-geometry audit promoted no global step or reversal in 12 cross-scope cells; an independently frozen pollinator-channel test was non-significant (W=1.619, df=3, p=0.655) and stopped before downstream mechanism testing; and an outcome-blind GloBI source-genus interaction-breadth analysis, matched for source availability and reference effort, promoted 0/4 context-by-floristic-stratum cells. Thus isolation does not simply intensify one floral island syndrome. It is associated with biogeographically distinct response fingerprints whose strongest global signal is expressed through lineage assembly, while the pollination mechanism responsible for that assembly remains unresolved.

**Keywords:** island biogeography; floral traits; reproductive assurance; source pool; lineage assembly; pollination; ecological filtering; hierarchical response; biogeographic contingency

## Introduction

Geographic isolation is one of the defining processes of island biogeography. Classical island theory treats separation from a regional source pool as a constraint on colonization opportunity, while empirical island biology has repeatedly identified characteristic changes in dispersal, life history, growth form and reproduction. In plants, these observations have motivated the idea of an “island syndrome”: a recurrent suite of traits associated with long-distance colonization, small population size, novel abiotic environments and altered biotic interactions.

Floral versions of the island syndrome commonly combine at least two biological arguments. The first is reproductive assurance. Long-distance colonists may have difficulty finding compatible mates or reliable pollen vectors, favouring self-compatibility, autonomous selfing or other forms of uniparental reproduction. This logic is closely related to Baker’s law and to later treatments of breeding system as a colonization filter rather than necessarily a repeated post-colonization evolutionary transition (Baker 1955; Pannell & Barrett 1998; Grossenbacher et al. 2017). The second argument concerns pollination-associated floral architecture. If island pollinator assemblages are species-poor, functionally altered or dominated by different visitor groups, the returns to conspicuous displays, restricted floral access, deep tubes or precise mechanical matching may change (Fenster et al. 2004; Rosas-Guerrero et al. 2014; Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson 2020).

These arguments are related but not equivalent. The selfing syndrome often includes reduced floral investment, yet self-compatibility is not autonomous selfing, autonomous selfing is not necessarily the dominant realized mating system, and a plant can gain reproductive assurance without losing specialized floral architecture. Conversely, floral attraction or accessibility traits can change without a detectable shift in reproductive assurance. Treating all of these outcomes as one syndrome therefore risks converting a broad assemblage pattern into a presumed causal sequence.

A second difficulty is that island floral patterns are assemblage patterns. A distant island may differ from a near island because different source-available lineages arrive, establish or persist, even if no lineage changes phenotype after colonization. This is the ecological logic behind interpreting Baker’s law partly as filtering of pre-existing reproductive variation. Recent work has similarly shown that breeding system, floral symmetry and arrival opportunity jointly influence island colonization, while explicit source-pool frameworks emphasize the need to distinguish trait filtering from generic island–mainland differences. Lineage composition should therefore not automatically be treated as a nuisance to be removed: it can be a biological outcome of island assembly.

This source-pool perspective changes the interpretation of geographic distance. We do not treat kilometres from a continent as a direct causal force on floral phenotype. Instead, distance is a composite axis of separation, connectivity and source accessibility. As separation increases, the opportunity for differential source-to-island filtering increases, but the biological response need not be one-dimensional. Different reproductive and floral components can respond in different directions, and an assemblage-level gradient can be generated at different taxonomic depths—from broad family sorting to genus representation to residual within-genus species composition.

The same geographic barrier may also act on the other side of plant–pollinator interactions. A pollination channel available in a regional source system must itself cross oceanic barriers, establish and persist before it can provide effective service on an island. Flight capacity, long-distance dispersal, stepping-stone use, habitat requirements and establishment probability can therefore make the same geographic distance a very different biological barrier for different functional groups. These considerations motivate a double geographic-filter framework in which isolation can jointly filter plant source-pool assembly and pollination-channel continuity. The present Chapter 1 directly evaluates the plant/source side; pollinator mechanism requires independent evidence rather than inference from floral phenotype.

A third challenge is inferential. Global island comparisons combine heterogeneous floristic histories, climates, island sizes, observation effort and taxonomic composition. A claim that one response is significant in one region and not another does not establish regional heterogeneity. Likewise, the disappearance of a signal after genus adjustment does not show how much of the response is associated with the genus transition, and an apparent breakpoint does not establish nonlinear biology unless that breakpoint could have been recovered reliably under the observed spatial structure. The scientific problem is therefore not only **whether** a response exists, but **where** it occurs, **which components** move, and **at what assembly level** the response is represented.

We addressed these problems with a progressive analysis contract. The island universe, trait ontology, hypothesis order, support thresholds, multiple-testing families, source/lineage safeguards and causal claim ceiling were fixed while trait evidence was allowed to improve. Each new trait snapshot was reanalysed under the same contract. The primary ladder asked: **H1**, whether increasing source separation generates one coherent floral/reproductive response vector; **H2**, whether response vectors instead branch among biogeographic contexts; **H3**, at what source/taxonomic depth a supported response remains; and **H4**, whether island area modifies isolation-associated filtering. Independent pollination evidence was reserved for a mechanistic gate rather than inferred from plant traits.

The primary H2 response is deliberately pollinator-name-free. We separate (i) **accessibility/generalization**, representing increasingly open, shallow or radially accessible floral architecture, from (ii) **reproductive assurance**, representing strict reproductive-assurance evidence. Only after the primary plant response is established do we evaluate fixed large-bee-like, butterfly-like and bird-like floral templates. These are pollination-associated architecture concordances, not visitor observations or pollinator classifiers.

Our central prediction is therefore not that distant islands should be uniformly selfing, generalized or inconspicuous. We ask three nested questions. First, **whether** source separation produces one coherent floral/reproductive response or different response directions among biogeographic contexts. Second, **where** those contrasting responses are expressed geographically and among floral/reproductive components. Third, **at what assembly level** a supported response remains after source-matched family and genus expectations are removed. A universal island syndrome predicts a common response direction; a context-dependent assembly model predicts component decoupling and potentially different hierarchical expression. We additionally tested, under separately calibrated identifiability gates, whether the broad global response contains reproducible nonlinear transitions and whether independent pollination proxies can explain the assembly pattern.

## Materials and Methods

### Geographic and taxonomic universe

The fixed geographic universe contained 8,265 islands. Species-level analyses used a fixed denominator of 106,295 accepted angiosperm species. The denominator was not allowed to shrink between trait waves. Island occurrences, floristic-status information, island area, climate covariates, geographic exposure and source-pool infrastructure were held constant while trait evidence was allowed to improve.

The primary geographic exposure was `log1p_distance_to_continent_km`. We interpret this as a composite of geographic separation, connectivity and source accessibility rather than as a mechanistically pure isolation treatment. The conceptual near-source limit represents high source accessibility and relatively weak oceanic filtering, not a claim that the nearest continent is the exact historical source of every island.

Analyses were conducted at two context layers. Broad analysis regimes included northern mid-latitude, northern high-latitude, Tropical and southern extratropical strata. Formal biogeographic realms included the Palearctic, Neotropical and other realm assignments where support permitted. We analysed `all_native`, `native_nonendemic` and `endemic` floristic strata where support thresholds were met. Persistence in native non-endemics was used to show that a pattern was not confined to island endemics; endemicity was not treated as a clock or as a direct estimator of in-situ evolution.

### Trait evidence and progressive wave integration

Each accepted species could contribute to three primary measurement domains: flower colour, floral structural complexity and reproductive assurance. The fixed denominator was therefore 318,885 species-by-axis cells. The final trait snapshot resolved 82,556 colour cells, 91,635 structural cells and 48,497 reproductive-assurance cells, for 222,688 resolved cells overall (69.83%).

Evidence precedence was fixed. Species-direct High/Medium evidence preceded trait-specific validated Low evidence. Family-level imputation, global fallback and post hoc threshold relaxation were prohibited. Missing evidence was never converted to trait absence. Reproductive evidence retained distinctions among self-incompatibility, mating system and autonomous selfing when source methodology permitted; self-compatibility alone was not mapped to autonomous or realized selfing.

All trait waves were materialized into the same `chapter1_trait_snapshot_v1` schema and reanalysed under `chapter1_progressive_analysis_v1`. Biological estimates were allowed to change as evidence improved, but model definitions, support gates and claim ceilings were not.

### Support gates and multivariate priority

Global fill fraction was descriptive and was never an inferential gate. Response-specific support was classified as <30 supported islands = not promoted, 30–49 = pilot, and >=50 = count component of confirmatory support. Pairwise context comparisons required the same response to meet the same support tier in both contexts. BH-FDR correction was applied within predeclared test families.

Multivariate response-vector tests preceded interpretation of individual axes. A significant slope in one context and a non-significant slope in another was not treated as evidence of heterogeneity without a direct between-context comparison.

### H1 — universal-syndrome rival

H1 asked whether increasing source separation generated one coherent floral/reproductive response vector across contexts. The primary test combined within-context multivariate support with direct between-context vector comparison. A universal syndrome was therefore rejected by direct response-vector heterogeneity, not by comparing significance labels among regions.

### H2 — biogeographic branching

H2 asked whether the primary plant response branched among prespecified biogeographic contexts. The pollinator-name-free response contained `accessibility_generalization` and `reproductive_assurance`. Analyses were repeated across evidence scopes and floristic strata. Climate-overlap analyses separately evaluated whether regional contrasts could be transported over common measured-climate support.

### Decomposing reproductive and floral components

To distinguish reproductive assurance from pollination-associated floral change, `selfing_core` included only self-incompatibility, mating-system and autonomous-selfing information and excluded floral attraction traits. A separate attraction/access pathway was constructed from floral architecture. Conditional models asked whether the isolation-associated attraction shift remained after conditioning on `selfing_core`; these analyses were treated as decomposition rather than causal mediation.

### H3 — source and lineage assembly

H3 asked at what taxonomic depth a supported H2 response remained. Outcome-blind source expectations were constructed using GIFT source flora and four fixed source definitions (`geo_k5`, `geo_k10`, `geo_k20`, and `geo50_climate10`). Taxonomic decomposition evaluated the response at three stages: observed assemblage response, residual after family composition, and residual after source-matched genus composition. Genus entry and within-represented-genus species loading were evaluated where support permitted.

Family and genus were grouping structures rather than trait-imputation devices. Disappearance after genus adjustment was interpreted as evidence that the broad response is represented by genus-level assembly; it was not interpreted as proof that propagule dispersal alone caused the filter.

### Effect-size attenuation across taxonomic depth

After the final Chapter 1 freeze, we re-expressed the already frozen H3 coefficients as an effect-size fingerprint without fitting new biological models. For the Palearctic primary two-axis response (`generalized_accessible` and `selfing_core`), we calculated the Euclidean norm of the two distance slopes at the observed, family-adjusted and genus-adjusted stages for each of 4 source definitions × 2 evidence scopes × 2 focal floristic strata. We report the fraction of the observed vector attenuated after family adjustment, after genus adjustment, and the additional attenuation introduced by genus adjustment relative to the family-adjusted remainder. These are descriptive attenuation quantities, not mediation proportions.

### Atomic component response fingerprint

We also re-expressed eight broad contrasts that were fixed before their original model coefficients were examined: `plain_colour`; `generalized_form`, `actinomorphic_symmetry`, `shallow_open_tube`, and `small_flower`; and `self_compatibility`, `selfing_mating_system`, and `autonomous_selfing`. Existing cluster-robust estimates and standard errors were converted to 95% intervals without generating a new inferential family. For visualization only, we treated the ordered eight coefficients as a response vector and calculated the angle between northern-midlatitude and Tropical vectors within each evidence-scope × floristic-stratum combination. The preregistered H1/H2 multivariate tests, not these descriptive angles, remain the inferential basis for regional heterogeneity.

### Matched context × taxonomic-stage audit

To avoid comparing unlike estimands across regions, a secondary post-freeze audit used the same four-component plant-architecture response before and after genus adjustment in northern-midlatitude and Tropical contexts. The target was a direct context × taxonomic-stage interaction, not a comparison of separate significance labels. The same four source definitions, both evidence scopes and the two focal floristic strata were retained. This audit was classified as a matched secondary re-expression of the frozen dataset rather than a new preregistered primary hypothesis.

### H4 — continuous area moderation

H4 used continuous distance-by-continuous-area models. No post hoc small/large island threshold was introduced. Equal-island, capped-information, direct-only, common-support and heteroskedastic-null safeguards were used to decide whether a directional interaction could be promoted beyond a measurement-sensitive modifier.

### Response-geometry identifiability audit

We separately asked whether broad colour, structural and reproductive contrasts showed identifiable nonlinear geographic responses. Before opening observed nonlinear results, five candidate geometries were fixed: flat, smooth/clinal, step, hinge and reversal. An initial recovery audit showed that naïve AICc selection could misclassify spatial-block variation as nonlinear structure, so observed geometry remained closed. A second design used separate calibration and validation simulations while preserving the empirical exposure distribution, trial counts, baseline covariates, spatial blocks and response-specific missingness. Only geometries that met the recovery and false-positive criteria were eligible for observed promotion. The observed data were then opened once under the frozen gate.

### Pollination-associated floral-architecture concordance

Only after the H2 plant response was established were fixed `large_bee_like`, `butterfly_like` and `bird_like` templates evaluated. These combined colour, form, symmetry, tube depth and size with prespecified weights. They were treated as floral-architecture concordances rather than pollinator observations. Source-trained factor decomposition quantified how much of the three-template covariance was shared plant architecture, and template-specific residuals were interpreted only after this common factor and source/taxonomic expectations were considered.

### Prospective independent pollinator-channel gate

The double-filter framework motivated a prospective test using independently constructed pollinator-channel exposure rather than floral phenotype. Before fitting island outcomes, the channel catalog and qualification rules were frozen. Bombus, non-Bombus bees, flower-visiting birds and Diptera qualified for the primary joint isolation × channel test; Lepidoptera did not provide disrupted-island support for that gate. The preregistered decision rule required significant joint channel heterogeneity before any downstream N2 mechanism test. No reduced-channel rescue or threshold retuning was permitted after the result was observed.

### Outcome-blind source-genus interaction-breadth extension

We additionally evaluated whether source-side plant interaction breadth could explain genus-level assembly without using island floral outcomes to construct the predictor. A pinned GloBI 0.9 SUPPORTS export was processed before any island occurrence or distance data were loaded. Explicit flower-interaction records were matched to the frozen plant taxonomy; unmatched taxa remained missing. We summarized each plant genus using functional-channel breadth across Bombus, non-Bombus bees, Lepidoptera, flower-visiting birds and Diptera. The primary breadth metric was the effective number of channels derived from independent reference counts; raw partner richness was not used, and genera without GloBI records were never coded as specialists.

The primary analysis required at least three independent references per genus, with two- and five-reference thresholds retained as fixed sensitivities. After the predictor table was frozen by SHA-256, island/source data were joined. Candidate source genera were matched on source prevalence, source species richness and a broad GloBI reference-effort class before calculating genus entry, species-weighted enrichment and loading. The primary target was the distance slope of effective-channel-number entry enrichment. This analysis was defined as D3 association evidence and could neither rescue the pollinator-channel gate nor open downstream mechanism testing.

### Robustness, missingness and stopping rule

Trait-resolution sensitivity used a finite predeclared MNAR grid rather than assuming missingness at random. Other layers evaluated common measured-climate support, source-definition sensitivity, direct-only evidence and information weighting. The final computational freeze was not based on reaching a target coverage percentage. We required a large increase in usable evidence without a corresponding change in the principal claim structure, declining realized yield from newly reviewed sources, and exhaustion of immediately promotable committed reproductive evidence under the strict evidence rules.

## Results

### One universal floral island syndrome was not recovered

The primary two-axis response did not recur as one coherent direction across context layers, evidence scopes and floristic strata. Response vectors differed among contexts, rejecting a simple model in which increasing source separation universally pushes island floras toward the same combination of greater reproductive assurance and greater floral generalization.

This conclusion was based on the frozen multivariate architecture rather than on comparing significance labels among regions. The principal result was therefore not that one trait “worked” in one region and failed in another, but that the multivariate response to source separation was context dependent.

### Source separation produced biogeographically distinct response directions

The Palearctic showed the clearest broad primary response. In all-analysis all-native assemblages, the accessibility/generalization slope was 0.0795 and the reproductive-assurance slope was 0.0534 per unit of log-distance; both were FDR-supported at q=0.00463. In direct-only evidence, the corresponding estimates were 0.0616 (q=0.0389) and 0.0966 (q=8.48×10^-14). The broad response persisted in native non-endemic assemblages, indicating that it was not confined to island endemics.

Tropical assemblages followed a contrasting trajectory. In direct-only all-native data, accessibility/generalization decreased with distance (-0.1005, q=0.00275) while reproductive assurance increased (0.1360, q=0.00428). Thus increasing isolation was compatible with stronger reproductive assurance without the floral-generalization response observed in the Palearctic.

These patterns establish context-associated branching, not a measured-climate-independent causal effect of realm identity. North–Tropical contrasts did not satisfy the outcome-blind common-support positivity requirement, and Palearctic–Neotropical climate-adjusted support did not reproduce consistently across both evidence scopes and both focal floristic strata.

### Regional response composition was not merely an amplitude difference

The eight-component response fingerprint showed that the regional contrast was not well described as one fixed syndrome becoming stronger or weaker. In northern-midlatitude assemblages, `actinomorphic_symmetry` had positive 95% intervals in both evidence scopes and both focal floristic strata. In Tropical native non-endemics, `plain_colour` and `autonomous_selfing` had positive intervals in both evidence scopes, while none of the four structural-complexity contrasts had an interval excluding zero in both scopes.

When the same eight components were treated as an ordered descriptive vector, the angle between northern-midlatitude and Tropical responses was 95.27° in all-analysis all natives, 100.21° in all-analysis native non-endemics, 116.49° in direct-only all natives and 99.53° in direct-only native non-endemics. Cosine similarity was negative in all four comparisons. These descriptive geometries reinforce the primary H1/H2 result: regional responses differ in component orientation, not only in magnitude.

The component decomposition also sharpened the distinction between reproductive assurance and floral simplification. Tropical native non-endemics could show plainer colour and stronger autonomous-selfing response without a parallel cross-scope shift across the four structural simplification contrasts. The data therefore do not support treating colour, structure and reproductive assurance as interchangeable manifestations of one latent island-simplification axis.

### The strongest Palearctic response was concentrated at the family-to-genus transition

Taxonomic-depth decomposition placed the broad Palearctic primary response at genus-level assembly. The compact frozen classification was `4/4 -> 4/4 -> 0/4`: the response was supported under all four source definitions at the observed stage, remained supported after family adjustment, and did not pass the post-genus vector gate after source-matched genus adjustment in either evidence scope or focal floristic stratum.

Effect-size attenuation showed where that transition occurred. Across 16 source-mode × evidence-scope × floristic-stratum profiles, family adjustment reduced the observed two-axis vector by 19.6–33.4% (median 26.2%). Source-matched genus adjustment reduced the original vector by 78.8–85.9% (median 81.3%). Relative to the family-adjusted remainder, the genus stage removed a further 70.6–79.1% (median 75.7%). Most attenuation therefore occurred between the family-adjusted and genus-adjusted stages rather than at the family stage.

This result does not mean that genus composition causally mediates approximately 80% of isolation effects. It means that the measured isolation-response vector is strongly reduced when source-matched genus composition is accounted for, localizing the dominant cross-sectional assemblage signal to the genus transition.

### Hierarchical expression itself differed among contexts, but within a bounded domain

The matched context × taxonomic-stage audit showed that genus adjustment changed the same four-component plant-architecture response differently between northern-midlatitude and Tropical contexts in widespread native assemblages. In native non-endemics, all four source definitions passed the frozen context × stage family in both all-analysis evidence (q range 0.00462–0.02166) and direct-only evidence (q range 0.00972–0.04921).

The all-native result was weaker: none of the four source definitions passed in all-analysis evidence, whereas three of four passed in direct-only evidence and the remaining source definition was borderline (q≈0.060). We therefore classify this as a **bounded hierarchical-depth signal**, not a universal law that every biogeographic context expresses isolation responses at a different taxonomic depth. Inclusion of island endemics weakens the matched pattern, but the current endemic support is insufficient to assign that weakening to in-situ evolution.

### Four simple explanation routes did not earn promotion

The main assembly result remained after several intuitive explanations were tested under independent promotion rules.

First, island area did not close the mechanism. All 16 frozen primary area classifications remained `retain_area_as_measurement_sensitive_modifier_only`; none passed the heteroskedastic-null promotion gate. Although 10/16 cells showed apparent small-island amplification across fitted sensitivities, the pattern could not be promoted to a founder, habitat-capacity or pollinator-persistence mechanism.

Second, the global response did not resolve into a reproducible nonlinear transition. Recovery calibration showed that a step response was identifiable in 11/12 cross-scope design cells, a reversal in 4/12 and a hinge in 0/12. After the observed data were opened once, all 12 cells were classified `monotonic_or_unresolved`; no step or reversal was promoted across both evidence scopes. Thus the absence of a global breakpoint was not simply a consequence of never testing nonlinear response geometry.

Third, the independently frozen pollinator-channel test was executable but did not support channel-specific isolation responses. The joint isolation × channel Wald statistic was W=1.6187 (df=3, p=0.65516), and leave-one-spatial-block-out and leave-one-source-region-out checks did not cross the frozen threshold. The preregistered chain therefore stopped before N2 without reduced-channel rescue or retuning.

Fourth, source-genus interaction breadth did not recover the genus-assembly pattern. The outcome-blind GloBI pipeline processed 24,577,183 raw interaction rows, retained 714,785 explicit flower-interaction rows, matched 524,922 evidence rows to the plant taxonomy and produced breadth estimates for 2,228 genera before any island outcome or distance was joined. After source prevalence, source richness and reference-effort matching, 0/4 context-by-floristic-stratum primary cells passed the frozen promotion rule. Representative primary slopes were near zero: for `geo50_climate10`, northern-midlatitude all natives had slope 0.0060 (95% CI -0.0092 to 0.0213; q=0.971), while Tropical all natives had slope 0.0077 (-0.0052 to 0.0207; q=0.361).

These negative gates do not prove that area, nonlinear dynamics or pollination are irrelevant. They show that the strong genus-level assembly signal is not trivially reducible to the specific tested versions of those explanations.

### Pollination-associated floral architecture remained an ecological compatibility layer

Secondary large-bee-like, butterfly-like and bird-like floral templates diverged among regions, but one source-trained common factor explained approximately 86.9% of variation among the three templates in all-analysis evidence and 86.4% in direct-only evidence. The simultaneous movement of named templates therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence that corresponding realized visitor guilds changed in parallel.

Some template-specific Tropical residual structure remained after source/genus adjustment, including positive large-bee-like residuals in robust comparisons. These remain plant-side architecture signals. They do not identify visitor identity, mobility, per-visit effectiveness or replacement.

### Progressive trait waves reached practical inference saturation

The final integrated snapshot contained 222,688 resolved species-by-axis cells (69.83%). Large additions of evidence late in the campaign no longer changed the major H1–H4 plant-side claim structure, while strict-source recovery entered strong diminishing returns. A residual audit found no immediately promotable reproductive records under the fixed evidence rules. We therefore treat the final snapshot as a defensible computational freeze based on inference stability and declining recoverability rather than on declaring 69.83% intrinsically sufficient.

## Discussion

### Isolation changes response composition, not simply syndrome strength

The global island flora does not support one floral/reproductive trajectory that strengthens monotonically with geographic separation. Instead, biogeographic context changes the composition of the response. The Palearctic combines increasing accessibility/generalization with increasing reproductive assurance, whereas Tropical direct-only assemblages combine increasing reproductive assurance with decreasing accessibility/generalization. The descriptive eight-component fingerprints make the same point at finer resolution: northern-midlatitude and Tropical vectors are approximately orthogonal to moderately opposed rather than collinear vectors of different length.

This distinction matters because floral island and selfing syndromes often bundle colour, structural accessibility and reproduction into one expected direction. Our data show that these components can decouple. In Tropical native non-endemics, plainer colour and autonomous selfing can strengthen with isolation without a parallel cross-scope shift across the four structural simplification contrasts. The useful ecological object is therefore not a scalar “syndrome strength”, but a multidimensional response fingerprint whose composition depends on context.

The vector-angle analysis is deliberately descriptive. It does not replace the frozen direct multivariate heterogeneity tests. Its value is explanatory: it shows why the same-significance-summary language is inadequate for the empirical pattern already established by H1/H2.

### Lineage assembly is concentrated at the genus transition

The strongest plant-side result is not merely that taxonomy matters. Effect-size attenuation localizes where it matters. Family adjustment removes roughly one-fifth to one-third of the Palearctic primary vector, whereas genus adjustment removes roughly four-fifths of the original response and about three-quarters of what remains after family adjustment. The principal transition is therefore between family and genus representation.

This makes the source-pool interpretation more specific. Geographic separation is associated with a different mix of source-available genera, rather than only with broad higher-taxonomic turnover or a robust primary response that persists unchanged within genera. Lineage composition is consequently not a nuisance covariate here; it is part of the biological response to island assembly.

At the same time, the attenuation fractions are not causal mediation estimates. A genus-level cross-sectional signal can integrate propagule dispersal, establishment, habitat filtering, demographic persistence, species loading within represented genera, historical contingency and interaction dependence. Nor does disappearance after genus adjustment prove that no within-lineage evolution occurs. It identifies the hierarchical scale at which the strongest measured global response is represented.

### Biogeographic context also changes hierarchical expression

The matched context × stage analysis extends this result beyond one region while preserving a strict boundary around the claim. Among native non-endemics, genus adjustment changes the same plant-architecture response differently between northern-midlatitude and Tropical contexts across all four source definitions and both evidence scopes. Thus context can alter not only the sign or mixture of ecological responses, but the hierarchical level at which the response remains visible.

The result is nevertheless bounded. It weakens in all-native assemblages, and Tropical endemic strata do not meet the support required to turn endemicity into a confirmatory time or evolutionary axis. We therefore do not equate a beyond-genus residual with in-situ evolution. Such residuals can include within-genus species sorting, unmeasured source composition, habitat structure and genuine evolutionary change. Distinguishing those possibilities requires phylogenetic or population-level data rather than stronger rhetoric around a cross-sectional residual.

### Simple mechanism shortcuts do not close the assembly explanation

The strength of the hierarchical result is clearer when the failed explanatory shortcuts are considered together. Apparent small-island amplification did not survive the frozen heteroskedastic-null mechanism gate. A nonlinear transition was testable for most step-response cells but no cross-evidence global step or reversal was promoted. The prospective pollinator-channel heterogeneity test failed before downstream mechanism testing. Finally, an independent plant-side GloBI breadth proxy did not explain isolation-associated genus representation after source availability and broad reference effort were matched.

These are not four interchangeable null results. Each removes a different shortcut. H4 prevents us from relabelling the genus pattern as a generic small-island capacity effect. The geometry audit prevents post hoc breakpoint storytelling. N1 prevents a direct claim that the tested pollinator channels differ globally in isolation response. The GloBI analysis prevents a simpler replacement claim that well-documented source genera with narrow functional-channel breadth are selectively lost with distance. What remains is a robust assembly-level pattern whose mechanism is not reducible to the currently tested coarse proxies.

This claim discipline is especially important for a large global dataset. With enough islands and traits, many biologically attractive post hoc decompositions can be generated. Here, the explanation space narrows because alternative routes were required to pass their own promotion gates rather than being accepted whenever they were narratively compatible with the main pattern.

### The double geographic-filter model remains a hypothesis space, not an identified mechanism

The combination of H2 and H3 still motivates a double-filter framework. Isolation clearly structures the plant/source side: source availability and genus representation are central to the strongest response. The same oceanic barrier can also filter interaction partners, and functional groups can differ in flight, dispersal, establishment and habitat dependence. A pollination-channel filter could therefore act upstream of genus assembly, such that its effect is absorbed when genus composition is controlled.

However, the present global analyses do not identify that route. N1 failed its prospective heterogeneity gate, and the effort-matched GloBI breadth proxy also failed promotion. These failures do not demonstrate that pollinators are unimportant; they demonstrate that **coarse global proxies are insufficient to identify the functional path**. A direct mechanism test requires source availability, realized visitation, per-visit pollen transfer and rate-weighted effective service measured independently of floral phenotype.

This also clarifies the role of pollination-associated floral templates. Large-bee-like, butterfly-like and bird-like scores are useful architecture coordinates, especially after their common factor is removed, but they are not observations of realized visitors. Their approximately 87% shared factor is itself a reason to avoid assigning pollinator identity from floral phenotype.

### Global smoothness and local thresholds are compatible

The calibrated response-geometry audit provides an additional scale boundary. We found no globally reproducible step or reversal across the broad colour, structural and reproductive contrasts even though step responses were identifiable in most design cells. This argues against reframing Chapter 1 around one global isolation threshold.

It does not imply that threshold dynamics cannot occur in particular plant–pollinator systems. A global assemblage averages across lineages, source histories, interaction partners and environmental settings, all of which can blur mechanism-specific regime changes. The appropriate next question is therefore local and functional: within one biological system, do floral, mating and autonomous-reproduction channels show different response geometries across independently observed pollination regimes, and do any thresholds co-localize with a measured change in effective service?

This is the bridge to Chapter 2. The global analysis establishes **where and whether** floral/reproductive responses branch and **at what assembly level** the strongest response is represented. The focal Campanula–pollinator system can then test **how and why** individual response channels change, including cline-versus-threshold alternatives that are not identifiable as a universal global rule.

### Progressive evidence growth shifted the bottleneck from traits to mechanism

The progressive trait campaign also supplies a stopping argument. The final snapshot increased usable evidence substantially relative to earlier waves without changing the main context-dependent and genus-assembly claim. At the same time, strict-source recovery entered diminishing returns. The remaining scientific uncertainty is therefore no longer primarily “how many more species can be assigned a trait?” but “what process produces the observed hierarchical filter?”

This distinction is practical as well as conceptual. Further indiscriminate trait filling is unlikely to transform the principal Chapter 1 inference. More informative work now comes from targeted mechanistic evidence, phylogenetic history and within-system functional tests.

---

## Conclusion

Increasing geographic separation does not impose one universal floral island syndrome. Island floras instead show biogeographically distinct response fingerprints: reproductive assurance, colour and floral structure can be re-weighted differently among contexts, and the hierarchical expression of those responses can also change.

The strongest Palearctic response is concentrated at the family-to-genus assembly transition. Source-matched genus adjustment attenuates approximately four-fifths of the primary response-vector magnitude, while a matched audit shows bounded context dependence in how genus adjustment changes floral architecture. Yet the genus-level pattern is not trivially explained by island area, one global nonlinear threshold, the prospectively tested pollinator-channel contrast, or an effort-matched source-genus interaction-breadth proxy.

The most defensible interpretation is therefore a source-pool and lineage-assembly account with an unresolved mechanism, not a universal causal syndrome. Chapter 1 closes the global pattern-and-hierarchy problem as far as the current data allow. The remaining mechanistic question belongs at a scale where interaction partners, effective pollen delivery and reproductive consequences can be measured directly.
