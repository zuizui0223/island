# Chapter 1 submission literature evidence — primary-source audit

**Checked:** 2026-08-27

**Purpose:** manuscript-ready background and Discussion support for the Chapter 1
`distance/connectivity × area/capacity → assemblage composition` framework.

**Source rule:** original papers and publisher, DOI, PubMed, or national-library
records only. Reviews are not used as the evidential endpoint. Rosas-Guerrero et
al. (2014) is retained because it is an original quantitative meta-analysis.

## Decision-ready claim map

| Chapter 1 statement | Evidence status | Safe manuscript wording | Claim ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isolation can lower colonization/immigration and area can raise persistence/carrying capacity | Established theory | Distance/connectivity and island area are expected to alter immigration and extinction opportunities | The classical theory predicts diversity dynamics, not floral traits, pollinator loss, or a specific source pool for any focal island |
| Distance also changes which mainland lineages can arrive | Theoretically well founded; historical source pools are usually latent | Mainland distance is a joint isolation/source-accessibility gradient | Distance alone cannot separate plant source-pool filtering from pollinator-channel filtering or reconstruct historical ancestry |
| Uniparental reproduction can help establishment by few founders | Original Baker hypothesis plus broad comparative support | Enrichment of reproductive-assurance traits is compatible with a Baker-type establishment filter | Self-compatibility (SC), autonomous selfing/autofertility, and realized selfing rate are not interchangeable; current SC does not identify founder number or post-colonization evolution |
| Floral traits can be compared with guild-associated syndromes | Supported only as probabilistic concordance | Trait combinations are compatible or incompatible with literature-defined pollination syndromes | Floral traits do not identify realized visitors, effective pollen transfer, historical pollinator absence, or replacement |
| A universal small/plain island flower syndrome is expected | Not supported by the strongest direct comparative study listed below | Island-mainland floral divergence is context dependent and may reflect assemblage filtering | Do not describe a universal evolutionary trajectory toward small or plain flowers |
| Bumblebee absence can accompany smaller flowers and reproductive-system change | Supported in the Izu *Campanula* system | The Izu studies provide a concrete, local example compatible with the proposed two-pathway mechanism | This is not a global test and cannot establish that Bombus loss caused the Palearctic Chapter 1 pattern |

## 1. Distance, area, and source-pool context

### MacArthur & Wilson (1963): isolation and area as joint assembly constraints

**Citation.** MacArthur, R. H., & Wilson, E. O. (1963). An equilibrium theory of
insular zoogeography. *Evolution*, **17**(4), 373–387.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.1963.tb03295.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.1963.tb03295.x)

**What the original paper supports.** Island species richness can be modelled as
a dynamic balance between immigration and extinction. Isolation enters through
colonization opportunity; area enters through the number and persistence of
populations. This is the foundational reason to model distance/connectivity and
area jointly rather than treating distance as a pure environmental driver.

**Boundary.** The model does not specify plant floral traits, pollinator movement,
effective pollination service, or a historical plant source pool. Chapter 1 must
therefore call distance a joint `isolation/source-accessibility` gradient unless
an independent source-pool denominator is supplied.

### Whittaker, Triantis & Ladle (2008): area/capacity is not temporally fixed

**Citation.** Whittaker, R. J., Triantis, K. A., & Ladle, R. J. (2008). A general
dynamic theory of oceanic island biogeography. *Journal of Biogeography*,
**35**(6), 977–994.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2699.2008.01892.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2699.2008.01892.x)

**What the original article supports.** Immigration, speciation, extinction, and
carrying capacity can vary with island ontogeny because island area, elevation,
and habitat opportunity change through time. It supports treating current area
as an ecological-capacity covariate, not as a complete history of opportunity.

**Boundary.** Present area is not historical area, founding population size, or
pollinator population viability. An observed `distance × area` interaction is
consistent with area-conditioned assembly but does not identify its mechanism.

## 2. Baker-type founder filtering and reproductive assurance

### Baker (1955): the original establishment argument

**Citation.** Baker, H. G. (1955). Self-compatibility and establishment after
“long-distance” dispersal. *Evolution*, **9**(3), 347–349.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.1955.tb01544.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.1955.tb01544.x)

**What the original paper supports.** A single self-compatible propagule can in
principle found a sexually reproducing population, whereas a self-incompatible
colonist requires compatible mates to arrive close enough in space and time.
This is a probabilistic establishment-filter hypothesis.

**Boundary.** It is not a global test and does not predict an area interaction,
measure historical founder number, demonstrate genetic drift, or distinguish SC
from autonomous selfing.

### Grossenbacher et al. (2017): strong but taxonomically bounded global evidence

**Citation.** Grossenbacher, D. L., Brandvain, Y., Auld, J. R., Burd, M., Cheptou,
P.-O., Conner, J. K., Grant, A. G., Hovick, S. M., Pannell, J. R., Pauw, A.,
Petanidou, T., Randle, A. M., Rubio de Casas, R., Vamosi, J., Winn, A., Igic, B.,
Busch, J. W., Kalisz, S., & Goldberg, E. E. (2017). Self-compatibility is
over-represented on islands. *New Phytologist*, **215**(1), 469–478.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.14534](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.14534)

**What the original analysis supports.** Across more than 1,500 species in
Asteraceae, Brassicaceae, and Solanaceae, 66% of island species versus 41% of
mainland species were classified as SC. The association survived genus-level
and several endemic-species sensitivity analyses. This is strong assemblage-level
evidence compatible with colonization filtering.

**Boundary.** It is a comparative association in three families, not observation
of colonization. The authors did not recover a simple greater SC frequency on
distant than near islands. It therefore cannot be cited as evidence that the
Chapter 1 distance gradient is specifically a Baker gradient.

### Razanajatovo et al. (2019): broader evidence, modest effect

**Citation.** Razanajatovo, M., van Kleunen, M., Kreft, H., Dawson, W., Essl, F.,
Pergl, J., Pyšek, P., Winter, M., & Weigelt, P. (2019). Autofertility and
self-compatibility moderately benefit island colonization of plants. *Global
Ecology and Biogeography*, **28**(3), 341–352.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12854](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12854)

**What the original analysis supports.** In 1,752 species from 161 angiosperm
families, higher SC and autofertility were associated with a slightly greater
probability of being an island colonist. The study explicitly distinguishes SC
from autofertility and characterizes the benefit as moderate.

**Boundary.** Selfing ability did not increase the probability of being island
endemic and did not differ between oceanic and continental island colonists.
The result supports a probabilistic founder filter, not universal selfing
evolution after arrival or an oceanic-island-specific effect.

### Zell et al. (2025): arrival opportunity and breeding system are separable

**Citation.** Zell, A. N., Miranda, C. H., Grady, E. L., Grossenbacher, D. L., &
Igić, B. (2025). Island colonization in flowering plants is determined by the
interplay of breeding system, lifespan, floral symmetry, and arrival opportunity.
*New Phytologist*, **245**(1), 420–432.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.20234](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.20234)

**What the original analysis supports.** Across 3,222 species from 169 families,
SC, lifespan, floral symmetry, and species-to-island distance all contained
information about island occurrence. The authors used distance from native
mainland occurrences to the nearest island as an explicit proxy for arrival
opportunity. Their best model supported arrival opportunity and breeding system
as important, partly independent filters.

**Boundary.** Species-to-island distance did not detectably interact with
breeding system, and the measured fixed effects explained only a small part of
total variation. Radial symmetry was itself only a proxy for generalized
pollination. This recent result directly supports keeping `arrival/source
opportunity`, `Baker-type breeding-system filtering`, and realized pollination
service as separate processes; it does not license a distance-amplified Baker
mechanism.

## 3. Pollination syndromes: useful concordance, invalid identification

### Ollerton et al. (2009): direct global test of predictive limits

**Citation.** Ollerton, J., Alarcón, R., Waser, N. M., Price, M. V., Watts, S.,
Cranmer, L., Hingston, A., Peter, C. I., & Rotenberry, J. (2009). A global test
of the pollination syndrome hypothesis. *Annals of Botany*, **103**(9),
1471–1480.
[https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcp031](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcp031)

**What the original study supports.** In six communities on three continents,
traditional discrete syndrome clusters poorly described phenotype space and the
nearest syndrome failed to predict the most common pollinator for approximately
two-thirds of plant species.

**Boundary.** A floral response vector must not be converted into a pollinator
identity. Chapter 1 can report syndrome *concordance* and discuss candidate
functional channels, but cannot infer realized or effective pollination service.

### Rosas-Guerrero et al. (2014): complementary meta-analytic evidence

**Citation.** Rosas-Guerrero, V., Aguilar, R., Martén-Rodríguez, S., Ashworth, L.,
Lopezaraiza-Mikel, M., Bastida, J. M., & Quesada, M. (2014). A quantitative
review of pollination syndromes: do floral traits predict effective pollinators?
*Ecology Letters*, **17**(3), 388–400.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.12224](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.12224)

**What the original meta-analysis supports.** Across 417 plant species, suites of
floral traits contained information about the most effective pollinator group;
predictability was higher in pollinator-dependent and tropical plants. This
supports using multivariate syndrome concordance as probabilistic ecological
context rather than dismissing the concept entirely.

**Boundary.** The result does not make any single trait diagnostic, does not
resolve historical changes, and does not imply that a trait-defined guild is
present on a focal island. Secondary pollinators were common, reinforcing the
need to avoid one-guild causal labels.

### Wang et al. (2020): island-community caution

**Citation.** Wang, X., Wen, M., Qian, X., Pei, N., & Zhang, D. (2020). Plants are
visited by more pollinator species than pollination syndromes predicted in an
oceanic island community. *Scientific Reports*, **10**, 13918.
[https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70954-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70954-7)

**What the original study supports.** In the Yongxing Island plant–pollinator
network, five floral traits explained 22.5% of visitation preference, and plants
were visited by more pollinator species than classical syndromes predicted.
Restrictive floral forms were more specialized, but the overall floral-trait
model did not identify functional pollinator groups.

**Boundary.** Visitors with pollen loads were potential pollinators; per-link
pollination efficiency and seed set were not quantified. This study directly
supports the Chapter 1 rule that syndrome matching is Discussion-level and not a
measure of effective service.

## 4. Island floral and reproductive shifts: original empirical examples

### Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson (2020): no universal flower-size syndrome

**Citation.** Hetherington-Rauth, M. C., & Johnson, M. T. J. (2020). Floral trait
evolution of angiosperms on Pacific islands. *The American Naturalist*,
**196**(1), 87–100.
[https://doi.org/10.1086/709018](https://doi.org/10.1086/709018)

**What the original analysis supports.** The study compared 556 species in 136
phylogenetically independent island–mainland sister-taxon contrasts across seven
Pacific island systems. Island flowers were not smaller on average; direction
varied among archipelagos and families. The authors explicitly identify
ecological filtering as a plausible explanation if small flowers are enriched
in island floras without consistent post-colonization size evolution.

**Boundary.** The study covers endemic taxa associated with tropical and
subtropical Pacific islands and tests flower size, not the full Chapter 1 trait
vector. It supports contingency and the assemblage/evolution distinction, not a
global mechanism.

### Inoue & Amano (1986) and Inoue (1988): a local Bombus-compatible case

**Citations.**

- Inoue, K., & Amano, M. (1986). Evolution of *Campanula punctata* Lam. in the
  Izu Islands: changes of pollinators and evolution of breeding systems. *Plant
  Species Biology*, **1**(1), 89–97.
  [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-1984.1986.tb00018.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-1984.1986.tb00018.x)
- Inoue, K. (1988). Pattern of breeding-system change in the Izu Islands in
  *Campanula punctata*: bumblebee-absence hypothesis. *Plant Species Biology*,
  **3**(2), 125–128.
  [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-1984.1988.tb00178.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-1984.1988.tb00178.x)

**What the original studies support.** Pollinator observations and breeding
experiments showed a mainland-to-Izu change from predominantly *Bombus* to
halictid-bee visitation, smaller island flowers, and a shift from strong SI on
Honshu toward SC/potential autogamy on islands lacking bumblebees. The expanded
1988 bagging survey across six islands reported SC on most islands where
bumblebees were absent and framed the pattern as a bumblebee-absence hypothesis.

**Boundary.** These studies provide a concrete system-level example of two
linked pathways—floral access/attraction change and reproductive assurance—but
they do not randomize pollinator loss, reconstruct every colonization history,
or test the global Palearctic assemblage. They can motivate, but cannot verify,
the Chapter 1 Bombus interpretation.

### Martén-Rodríguez et al. (2010): alternative channels and reproductive assurance can coexist

**Citation.** Martén-Rodríguez, S., Fenster, C. B., Agnarsson, I., Skog, L. E., &
Zimmer, E. A. (2010). Evolutionary breakdown of pollination specialization in a
Caribbean plant radiation. *New Phytologist*, **188**(2), 403–417.
[https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2010.03330.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2010.03330.x)

**What the original study supports.** Phylogenetic reconstruction of Antillean
Gesnerieae found repeated origins of bat pollination and generalized pollination
(bats, moths, and hummingbirds), correlated floral transitions, and autonomous
self-pollination in several hummingbird-pollinated lineages. Direct ecological
work in the system linked specialized lineages to low visitation and pollen
limitation.

**Boundary.** This is one Caribbean radiation. It demonstrates that
generalization, alternative guilds, and autogamy are biologically plausible
branches, not that birds, bats, or moths replace lost Bombus globally. It also
shows why `plain/generalized` and `SC` should not be collapsed into a single
syndrome.

## 5. Manuscript-ready synthesis

### Background sentence

> Classical island biogeography predicts that isolation limits immigration while
> island area modifies population persistence and carrying capacity; for floral
> and reproductive traits, however, mainland distance is best treated as a joint
> isolation and source-accessibility gradient because it does not reveal which
> plant lineages or pollination channels were historically available.

### Baker interpretation

> The enrichment of reproductive-assurance traits on smaller or more isolated
> islands is compatible with a Baker-type establishment filter, under which
> uniparental reproduction can increase establishment probability when propagules
> or mates are scarce. This interpretation does not identify historical founder
> number, genetic founder effects, pollinator loss, or post-colonization evolution.

### Pollination-syndrome interpretation

> Multivariate floral traits can be compared with literature-defined pollination
> syndromes as probabilistic functional concordance, but syndrome matching cannot
> identify realized visitors, effective pollen transfer, historical absence, or
> guild replacement. The observed Chapter 1 response vectors therefore motivate
> candidate pollination-channel explanations only at Discussion level.

### Universal-syndrome conclusion

> Direct island–mainland comparisons reject a universal reduction in flower size
> and instead show archipelago- and lineage-dependent divergence. Together with
> the Chapter 1 lineage results, this makes assemblage filtering and regional
> contingency the defensible main interpretation; post-colonization floral
> evolution remains an unmeasured alternative.

## 6. Explicit statements not licensed by this evidence set

- “Bombus loss caused the Palearctic response vector.”
- “Birds, butterflies, moths, or bats replaced Bombus in tropical or southern
  islands.”
- “Distance measures pollinator dispersal limitation rather than plant source-pool
  accessibility.”
- “Self-compatibility proves autonomous selfing, reproductive assurance, or a
  genetic founder effect.”
- “A pollination syndrome identifies the effective pollinator of a species.”
- “Island isolation universally drives flowers toward smaller, plainer, or more
  generalized forms.”
