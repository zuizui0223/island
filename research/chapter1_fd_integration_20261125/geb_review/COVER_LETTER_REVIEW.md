**Why this paper matters to GEB readers (under 250 words):**

Islands are replicated natural settings for testing how geographic isolation shapes biological assemblages. Yet treating islands as environments that must produce one inconspicuous, selfing-associated flower type overlooks variation among regional floras and the distinct roles of reproductive and floral traits. We analysed seven separately measured reproductive and floral traits across 4,379 sampled islands in four geographic regions, drawing on a 106,295-species trait database. Self-compatibility was positively associated with isolation in all four regions, whereas floral structure, colour and other components of reproductive assurance responded less uniformly. After accounting for measured reproductive assurance, accessibility-related associations remained in selected regions. In an independent global compilation of 2,969 pollen-supplementation experiments, pollen limitation was positively associated with distance from continents, including within offshore sites. Exact-species, post-hoc comparisons linked reproductive-assurance and floral-accessibility scores to lower present-day pollen limitation. A post-hoc analysis of paired Direct records across 257 tropical island floras further found a depth-dependent symmetry response that persisted under several reproductive adjustments. This clarifies the dimensionality of floral assembly, without claiming uniform evidence-grade or native robustness. The paper reframes island syndromes as recurrent reproductive traits alongside contingent floral combinations. We distinguish these comparative patterns from causal evidence of pollinator decline or trait evolution.

The analyses reuse previously published occurrence, trait and pollen-supplementation evidence, and the full trait ledger includes third-party records with heterogeneous redistribution permissions.
