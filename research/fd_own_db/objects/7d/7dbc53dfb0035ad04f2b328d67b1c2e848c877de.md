# Geographically contingent reassembly of floral trait combinations on islands and their association with potential pollinator diets

Working manuscript, 24 November 2026. **Exploratory observational study; not submission-ready.** Numerical results are source-verified. A targeted literature comparison, source-linked methods, and five reproducible aggregate-data draft figures are available. Original GIFT version/primary source attribution, full methods for archived global geography runs, rights review and final journal typesetting remain unfinished. This draft uses the global botanical data as the primary population and gives the independently compiled animal analyses a central Results section, rather than treating them as proof of the botanical mechanism.

## Abstract

Island floras are often expected to exhibit reduced floral specialization as pollinator assemblages become impoverished. Yet the functional consequences of isolation may depend on the composition, rather than simply the number, of potential interaction partners. We examined geographical reassembly of floral trait combinations in a global botanical frame of 4,505 island floras and evaluated exploratory associations with independently assembled bee, bird and butterfly records. Among 2,324 floras with directly documented flower colour and form, the same combinations recurred even between islands sharing no accepted plant species or genera. Relative to candidate mainland pools, actinomorphic × deep-tube frequency was 10.99 percentage points higher on tropical islands (666 floras) but lower at northern high latitudes. In the tropics, zygomorphic × deep frequency was lower by 1.37 points. The opposite-sign joint-frequency and covariance contrasts persisted in a nonrandom, documented-native subset (96 floras). Tropical covariance differences were predominantly associated with between-genus components. The positive tubular/bell × deep frequency contrast co-occurred with a negative covariance contrast. In an independent animal-data integration, the specialist-minus-partial nectar-associated bird coefficient for tubular flower form was positive across all 11 estimable, nested sensitivity panels. Bird-family-conditioned dietary permutations supported this association in several panels, but genus-conditioned permutations, plant-side taxonomy controls and native-status sensitivity did not establish robust functional matching. On identical jointly scored plant cohorts, depth and form differed in their taxonomy-conditioned residuals. These observations motivate a distinction between geographical assembly of potential partners and the resource-access traits that may govern their interactions. Botanical native-subset robustness and failure to establish a native diet association are different results on different analytical frames. Floral reassembly on islands is geographically contingent; changes in realized pollination and adaptive floral evolution remain unverified.

## Introduction

### 1. From an island floral syndrome to geographically contingent trait combinations

Biogeographical processes influence the composition and diversity of island floras. A functional island biogeography framework already predicts changes in mutualist diversity, reproductive strategy and pollination-associated traits with isolation (Schrader et al., 2021). Comparative evidence also challenges universal floral miniaturization: island–mainland sister-taxon contrasts show context-dependent flower-size evolution (Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson, 2020). Within Malesia, floral trait occurrence varies with climatic and geographical gradients (Trethowan et al., 2024), and multiple reproductive and floral traits jointly predict island occurrence at the species level (Zell et al., 2025). The general premise that island filtering is trait-dependent is therefore established.

A distinct assemblage-level question concerns the joint distribution of traits rather than their marginal frequencies or a scalar measure of functional diversity. Consider binary indicators A for actinomorphy and D for a deep floral tube. An island may differ from its candidate source in the frequency of A, the frequency of D, and the tendency for these states to occur together in the same documented plant species. The identity J = E[AD] = E[A]E[D] + Cov(A,D) separates a joint-frequency shift from the contribution of the association between the traits. The two components must not be conflated. In particular, nominal additive trait diversity can change little while the frequency of a particular joint category changes. The relevant question is whether the direction of joint floral reassembly and its covariance component differ among geographical regions and among combinations sharing a resource-access trait. Symmetry can affect the geometry of visitor approach and depth can affect resource access, but neither categorical state is a validated unidimensional measure of pollination specialization. We frame a hypothesis of architecture-dependent reassembly, rather than assume an ordering of specialization across all combinations.

### 2. Assemblage composition as a source of trait associations

Reconstructing island trait spaces is not a new goal. Barajas Barbosa et al. (2023) related the functional diversity of the Tenerife native flora to biogeographical and diversification history. Song et al. (2026) compared structural, hydraulic and carbon-related traits in Chinese island and mainland tree assemblages and documented differences between species- and community-scale trait associations. These findings provide a direct precedent for distinguishing changes in trait values from changes in their associations across organizational scales.

Here we extend that question to documented pollination-associated categorical phenotypes across a global island-flora frame, with potential mainland species pools as comparators. Conditional on a plant genus G, the law of total covariance partitions Cov(A,D) into Cov(E[A|G],E[D|G]) and E[Cov(A,D|G)]. We use the island–source contrast in these components to distinguish the contribution of genus composition and between-genus differences in category frequencies from residual within-genus associations. This is an application of a standard statistical identity, not a new decomposition method or a reconstruction of evolutionary change. Repeated floral categories across taxonomically disjoint assemblages provide a complementary descriptive result, not evidence for evolutionary convergence beyond a null.

### 3. Potential partner assembly versus realized interaction matching

Local field evidence already demonstrates the importance of pollinator function and composition. Hiraiwa and Ushimaru (2024) linked pollinator functional diversity to measured trait matching and reproductive success across coastal networks. Ishii et al. (2025) related colour, symmetry and tube length to pollinator fauna in six geographically distinct plant communities, accounting for plant family composition. Abrahamczyk (2019) compared generalist bird-pollinated plants between islands and continents. Thus partner identity, dietary differentiation, resource-access morphology and plant taxonomic composition are established subjects, not novel concepts in isolation.

Our question is whether the geographically varying combinations identified in the botanical analysis are accompanied by corresponding variation in independently recorded potential partners. We contrast the associations of nectar-associated bird diet classes with recorded floral form and depth, and use plant-side and bird-side taxonomy-conditioned expectations to ask whether apparent correspondence exceeds what their observed incidence and taxonomic structure permit. Neither island-level co-occurrence nor a species-wide diet fraction establishes local visitation or pollen delivery. We therefore separate geographical occupancy, resource dependence and possible functional matching as conceptual filters, without treating the three as an identified causal sequence.

### 4. Study questions and contribution

We ask four questions:
1. Do documented floral trait combinations recur between taxonomically dissimilar island floras?
2. Does the direction of joint floral reassembly relative to candidate mainland pools differ among macroregions, and how much of the focal tropical covariance contrast lies between versus within genera?
3. Do independently compiled potential pollinator assemblages show dietary and resource-use differentiation in relation to island geography and floral composition?
4. Do observed floral–diet associations exceed conditional expectations under plant taxonomy/record quality and animal taxonomy, separately and jointly?

The empirical contribution is the combination of geographically contrasting joint floral distributions, explicit genus accounting of the tropical covariance contrast, and an independent but geographically restricted animal-association analysis with two-sided nulls. The first two questions concern the global botanical study; the latter two are exploratory mechanism diagnostics. These are retrospectively organized study questions, not a newly preregistered confirmatory test. We do not claim a universal syndrome, an unprecedented analytical identity, or demonstrated pollinator-mediated evolution.

## Materials and methods

### Study design and botanical frame

We used an archived, project-specific island-flora reconstruction derived from the Global Inventory of Floras and Traits (GIFT; Weigelt et al., 2020; Denelle et al., 2023) and a separate physical-island reference derived from the Global Self-consistent, Hierarchical, High-resolution Geography database (GSHHG; Wessel & Smith, 1996, 2017). The frozen reconstruction contains 4,505 botanical island floras and the reference geography contains 8,264 island entities. These are the denominators of the project-specific analytical frames, not the total number of geographical regions in the current public GIFT release. We retained the full botanical frame in population accounting, with endpoint-specific eligibility; no complete-case filter across all traits was imposed. The exact original GIFT release, primary checklist references and original geographical reconstruction recipe require final provenance reconciliation before submission; see the separate source registry. The archived tropical regime includes 1,558 botanical floras.

We retained unique accepted-species × island combinations, with the original documented native, introduced and unresolved origin labels. Trait records were drawn from the frozen project-curated Direct evidence ledger, not inferred from a genus mean or a pollination syndrome. Single-category analyses used species-Direct high/medium evidence and unambiguous single-state records; high-only sensitivity retained high evidence. Joint form/symmetry/depth or colour/form outcomes required the corresponding observations for the same accepted species. Joint quality was classified as high only when both traits had high-grade evidence; otherwise it was medium. Unknown or conflicting records were not converted to an absent trait. Traits documented for a species were associated with its island occurrence; the data do not measure its phenotype locally on that island.

### Floral repertoire overlap and candidate mainland comparison

For the global colour × form comparison, 2,324 botanical floras contained at least five accepted species with documented same-species trait combinations. For two eligible floras i,j with category-frequency vectors p_i,p_j, overlap was O_ij = 1 − (1/2)Σ_k |p_ik − p_jk|. Each vector was based on scored species rather than abundance. We summarized all unordered pairs, pairs with no common accepted species, and the more restrictive group with no shared certified genus in the entire recorded island floras. Uncertain genus assignments were excluded from strict genus-disjoint certification. Pairwise overlaps share islands and are not independent replicates. We retained the original fixed-mask taxonomy-preserving reference for the squared category-frequency distance; overlap alone does not demonstrate above-null convergence.

For the symmetry × depth endpoint we defined A and D as binary indicators of the documented actinomorphic and deep tube categories, respectively. For each scored roster, J = E[AD], p_A = E[A], p_D = E[D], and C = J − p_A p_D. The difference between an island and a candidate source was ΔJ = J_island − J_source; analogously ΔC was calculated separately. The law of total covariance gives C = Cov_G(E[A|G],E[D|G]) + E_G[Cov(A,D|G)], with genus weights equal to each genus's fraction of scored species in the roster. We subtracted the source terms from the island terms to obtain the between- and within-genus components of ΔC. These are exact descriptions of current species mixtures, not an evolutionary or causal partition.

The protected source archive includes 748 candidate native mainland regional species lists. We used its original source assignments: five geographically nearest candidate regions (geo5), or ten climate-similar candidate regions drawn from a geographically restricted set (climate10; original geo50_climate10 assignment). For the animal-matched replay, each candidate source was the union of the assigned regional lists after excluding the complete recorded focal island species roster, including unscored species, then restricting to the same Direct trait eligibility definition used on the island. Both source and target needed at least five scored species, with stricter target support as specified below. Candidate source regions are present-day reference pools, not demonstrated historical donors. In the archived global same-species pair comparisons (distinct from the later animal-matched replay), each target required at least five jointly scored species and each of the two candidate sources at least ten jointly scored distinct species. Eligibility required both source definitions on the identical target-island cohort. Region means were calculated with equal weight per 10° geographical block; the original archived analysis also used first candidate GIFT source-group aggregation and a documentation-balanced subset requiring the island and both sources to differ by no more than 10 percentage points in trait annotation fraction. The original genus-covariance report specifies 999 whole-10°-block resamples; geology/LGM results use separate 999-draw whole-geography and first-source-group bootstraps. These intervals remain conditional exploratory summaries. Primary checklist and complete original source-acquisition provenance still need reconciliation.

### Independent potential-partner datasets and geographical matching

For bees we used the published island checklist, original island geometry and model table of Marshall et al. (2026; data DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23222636). The source reports 4,128 native species from 305 islands; our analyses used the source native richness field FinalRichNat, rather than counting raw names as accepted species. Source geometry had 309 records for the 305 model IDs. Three IDs with multiple geometry records were excluded, leaving 302 unique candidates. An island geometry was matched uniquely to the reconstructed GSHHG reference only if the intersection covered at least 90% of each polygon's area. This produced 234 matches; 231 could be joined to the historical public floral aggregate, including 130 tropical candidates. Unmatched geometries were not substituted using the nearest centroid. Bee source richness is not a proxy for Bombus alone or for effective pollination.

Bird species-by-island matrices were taken from Matthews et al. (2023), pinned to the accompanying DARs source archive. We used the contemporary true-island checklist subset, not the original study's habitat-island or extinct/introduced alternatives. Bird names in the source BirdTree taxonomy were joined to EltonTraits (Wilman et al., 2014) and the AVONET species-average BirdTree table (Tobias et al., 2022; pinned secondary CSV mirror), using exact normalized names. The original bird-matrix richness footer and diet-class counts were replayed. We defined three mutually exclusive categories using the source Diet.Nect percentage: ≥50%, >0 to <50%, and 0%. This variable includes nectar, pollen and exudates; the labels “specialist”, “partial” and “other” are shorthand for these diet fractions, not measured pollination specialization. Bird island matches used normalized or explicitly enumerated island names, matching archipelago and source area ratios 0.8–1.25 without reused IDs. These are provisional candidates rather than independently verified equivalent polygons. Sensitivity subsets required normalized names and ratios 0.9–1.1 or excluded two questionable shared-boundary units. The bird–bee bridge comprised 52 candidates, of which 38 were tropical (seven source archipelagos, six informative for within-group contrasts). The matched tropical bird incidence comprised 377 distinct names; the separate wing-shape analysis covered 497 distinct species across ten tropical candidate archipelagos. Hand-wing index (HWI) was interpreted as a flight-morphology proxy, not realized movement.

Butterfly occurrences were obtained from Yau et al. (2025), using the exact revised occurrence CSV identified by Figshare file 66997676, with 747,825 raw rows; the publication describes a different earlier total of 730,190. We used accepted-name and coordinate fields from the archived table and assigned valid points to unique original bee polygons. The geographical subset extended across tropical and peripheral Asian/Pacific islands. We retained positive observations only and distinguished all records, nominal coordinate uncertainty ≤1 km, and records lacking a GBIF identifier but including a source reference. The latter is a source-sensitivity mask, not proof of independent non-GBIF sampling. A point's nominal uncertainty does not guarantee its uncertainty disk lies inside the assigned island. Recorded species counts are not complete native richness; no unrecorded species or islands were assigned true absences. Butterfly wing-size and documented larval host-family counts came from the pinned LepTraits consensus (Shirey et al., 2022); ambiguous trait-name matches were excluded. Host documentation count was evaluated separately from host-family breadth. Further details and exact archive identifiers are in the source registry.

### Exploratory geographical and floral–diet associations

For the 38 tropical bird–bee candidate units, we compared within-archipelago, equally group-weighted slopes of log1p species counts with log2(1 + distance in km), adjusted for log2 island area. We compared the broad nectar-associated category against native bees and nonnectar birds. Mainland distance, distance to land >1 km², and distance to land >1,000 km² were assessed in separate models. The resulting contrasts are differences between regression slopes, not fixed-total substitution effects; the distance definitions are correlated and cannot isolate independent dispersal pathways.

For recorded floral-form associations, the discovery analysis used a frozen historical public colour/form aggregate with name units and documented category state sets. The tubular endpoint indicates documented membership of the tubular form category; extended-access form includes tubular, salverform, funnel/trumpet and spurred categories. Neither is a measure of physical tube depth. We considered four documentation scopes (Direct or single-state Direct, ≥5 or ≥50 scored names) and three geographic match masks (all, tight, exclude questionable shared boundaries): 12 nested comparisons, 11 estimable. For island i in archipelago g, the design contained natural-log-transformed counts log(1 + B_i), log(1 + S_i), log(1 + P_i), log(1 + O_i) for native bees, ≥50% diet, partial diet and zero diet, respectively, together with log2(area), log2(1 + mainland distance) and log2(scored plant names). All design and response columns were centered within groups, then weighted by 1/√n_g, where n_g is the number of islands in that group, giving equal total group weights. Singleton groups did not contribute a within-group contrast. Fits required full design rank and at least four residual degrees of freedom; fewer than 30 informative islands or ten independent groups failed the planned primary support criterion and were treated as exploratory even when estimable.

We tested β_S − β_P by reparameterizing the two diet-count covariates (or, in the protected analyses, as a direct linear coefficient contrast). This is not an effect of replacing one bird species by another at a fixed total count. Group resampling (499 draws, rank-valid fits only) and leave-one-group fits quantified sensitivity; percentile ranges are not treated as calibrated confidence intervals. Held-out comparisons fitted the models to the remaining archipelagos and compared squared prediction error for the centered contrast within the omitted group, using the same eligible groups. The held-out outcome mean defines the contrast being evaluated, not a training input. This does not assess absolute predictions for new islands.

### Original species-Direct replay, conditional nulls and exact taxonomy accounting

The protected original analysis repeated the comparisons on the 4,505-flora parent using the actual single-state Direct floral_form, tube_depth_class and paired form/depth records, not the historical name-unit aggregates. The original analysis included all-recorded, literal-native and high-only subsets, target thresholds of five or 30 scored species, and both mainland references. Source-relative models additionally included log2 of the candidate source's scored species count. To isolate the influence of recording different traits, we compared full form support, form restricted to the islands shared with the paired endpoint, and the genuine same-species form–depth roster on those same islands. The two shared-island panels used the same paired-roster controls.

For animal-side randomization we fixed observed island–bird incidence and permuted the three diet labels coherently across distinct species within a family or genus, preserving the label counts of each taxonomic block. The public discovery analysis used 1,999 shuffles for each of four documentation scopes × three outcomes × two taxonomic block definitions, yielding 24 exploratory directional tests; BH adjustment was applied over all 24. For plant-side randomization we fixed plant incidence and jointly permuted the species-level states within genus × evidence-quality blocks, including the same shuffled state wherever that accepted species occurred in target or source. The original form/depth analysis used 399 draws, with two-sided permutation statistics and BH across 500 tests. This is not a phylogenetic model or a test of local phenotypic evolution.

For the original dual-null analysis, we used the exact bird species pool and plant target/source species union for each of 24 nested panels. We tested plant-only, bird-only and independently joint shuffles (999 draws). Bird labels were shuffled separately within family and genus; fits were re-estimated after label permutation. A comparison required movable blocks on each side and at least 90% rank-valid permutation fits. A positive-direction Monte Carlo p value was (1 + number of null statistics ≥ observed)/(1 + number of valid draws). The intersection–union statistic was the maximum of the three p values: support required rejecting all three conditional nulls. Holm adjustment across 160 qualified comparisons controlled the familywise error for that exploratory test family; no panel attained the prespecified independent-group support gate. These comparisons were targeted following earlier exploratory patterns, not independent replication.

For the exact plant taxonomy diagnostic, we replaced each species' state vector by the mean vector of its genus × evidence-grade block on the distinct-species union of target and source rosters. This is the conditional expectation of the uniform within-block state shuffle. For fixed contrast weights w, the observed coefficient is wᵀY = wᵀE_null(Y) + wᵀ[Y − E_null(Y)]. The residual is defined statistically and is not a within-species phenotype or causal effect. A separate strict name-disjoint mainland donor audit required ≥2 independent donor species per genus(/grade), excluded all recorded plants from all matched target islands, and required ≥5 supported species and ≥50% target coverage per island; no eligible model resulted.

### Origin uncertainty

We decomposed the change from the all-recorded to the literal-native analysis into (i) restriction to the identical native-supported island cohort, (ii) change from all-recorded to native scored-count controls, and (iii) change in the plant roster. With fixed genus-grade expected states, the last component was exactly partitioned into the known introduced and unresolved-origin contributions, weighted by their fractions of the original recorded flora. These changes depend on the chosen order and model; they are not interventions. Finally, for a fixed design and scored flora, we retained known natives, excluded known introduced species and allowed arbitrary subsets of unresolved-origin scored species to be native, requiring at least five scored natives. We calculated endpoint-wise extremes over admissible subset sizes with per-island caps of 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of unresolved records and combined extremes using the fixed signed regression weights. These conditional identification bounds are not sampling confidence intervals or jointly attainable across endpoints. We did not impute native origin or refit unknown-dependent richness covariates. All analyses retained unsupported cases in the evidence records.

## Results

### R1. Floral functional repertoires recur despite taxonomic turnover (Fig. 1)
In 2,324 island floras with at least five directly scored colour × form plant taxa, 2,699,326 pairwise comparisons gave mean frequency overlap 0.386901. Among 842,058 pairs without a shared accepted species, overlap remained 0.247183. Among 155,569 strictly genus-disjoint pairs, overlap was 0.214879. These results show recurrence of documented floral categories across different assemblages; they do not demonstrate convergence above the taxonomy-preserving null. [Evidence A]

### R2. Joint floral reassembly differs among regions and among deep-tube architectures (Fig. 2)
Relative to candidate mainland plant pools, actinomorphic × deep-tube joint frequency was elevated by 10.989 percentage points in the tropics (666 islands; physical-block interval +8.958 to +13.313). The corresponding estimates were +2.580 points in northern midlatitudes (1,443 islands; +1.064 to +4.131), +4.103 in the southern extratropics (118 islands; +0.697 to +7.533), and −2.704 in northern high latitudes (135 islands; −3.562 to −1.895). The four regional estimates derive from the original geographical analysis, not the distinct three-region nominal-Q evaluation. Among 123 animal-matched tropical islands, the positive contrast persisted under both geo5 (+9.52 points) and climate10 (+9.41 points). This is a nested cohort sensitivity, not independent replication.

In the tropical geo5 analysis, joint-frequency point differences were +10.98858 points for actinomorphic × deep, −1.368 points for zygomorphic × deep (666 islands for each symmetry–depth outcome), and +5.172 points for tubular/bell × deep (598 islands; a distinct eligible roster). The latter flower-form category is not synonymous with the deep class and may overlap either symmetry state. We do not sum these overlapping outcomes or interpret their point differences as a test of net depth prevalence. The archived 95% block intervals were −2.68 to −0.05 for zygomorphic × deep and +3.82 to +6.61 for tubular/bell × deep. These are conditional exploratory intervals, not simultaneous tests.

Marginal-corrected covariance provides complementary evidence. Tropical actinomorphic × deep ΔC was +4.01790 points (conditional geographical-block interval +3.1737 to +4.9234), with between-genus +4.05652 and within-genus −0.03862. Zygomorphic × deep ΔC was −3.4253 points (−4.3149 to −2.5685), with between-genus −3.6390 and within-genus +0.2137. Tubular/bell × deep ΔC was −1.0973 points (−1.9404 to −0.2753), with between-genus −1.6341 and within-genus +0.5367. Thus, a combination can become more frequent even when its marginal-corrected association decreases. The radial/deep joint-frequency contrast partitions arithmetically as +10.98858 = +6.97068 (product-of-marginals difference) + 4.01790 (covariance difference), with ΔC itself partitioned into the between- and within-genus terms above. The within-genus components are not identified within-lineage transitions: no tropical radial/deep flora had a common variable genus across island and both candidate source definitions.

In the original literal-known-native symmetry/depth subset (96 tropical floras, 26 first-source groups), the opposite-sign pattern remained: radial × deep ΔJ +12.63 points [+8.64,+17.11] and ΔC +7.08 [+5.41,+8.68]; bilateral × deep ΔJ −6.58 [−8.82,−4.17] and ΔC −6.60 [−8.20,−4.94] (Fig. 2D). First-source grouping retained positive radial ΔJ (+9.92 [+5.46,+14.45]) and ΔC (+6.89 [+5.16,+8.72]) and negative bilateral ΔC (−6.47 [−8.42,−4.71]). These estimates describe the nonrandom documented-native roster, not a matched intervention or an effect of native status. The all-recorded radial/deep pattern was also positive under climate10 (ΔJ +11.07 [+9.10,+13.33]; ΔC +4.47 [+3.67,+5.33]), first-source-group averaging (geo5 ΔJ +7.32 [+5.07,+9.52]; ΔC +2.76 [+1.58,+3.97]) and the n=435 documentation-balanced cohort (geo5 ΔJ +9.66 [+7.85,+11.35]; ΔC +3.46 [+2.48,+4.42]). The latter controls the difference in trait-annotation fraction, not unknown state-dependent recording biases (Fig. 2E). None is independent replication.

Separate geology and LGM contrasts were +6.69 and +6.90 points for radial × deep (oceanic 124 vs continental 37; unconnected 347 vs connected 257). LGM unconnected − connected was −3.62 points for zygomorphic × deep (−5.91 to −1.43). Geological origin and LGM history were not independently identifiable because the jointly labelled tropical radial/deep cohort lacked the oceanic-connected cell (Fig. S1). These comparisons and the regional contrasts support an architecture-specific assemblage description, not identified pollinator selection, changes in total specialization, or native-only adaptation. [Evidence B–C]

### R3. Geographical differentiation of potential interaction partners (Fig. 3)
The independent bee/flower crosswalk comprised 234 unique geometries; 231 matched the historical public floral aggregate. The bird–bee dataset contained 38 provisional tropical candidate islands in seven archipelagos, six of which were informative in within-group analyses. Relative to bees, the mainland-isolation slope for all nectar-associated birds was +0.256; relative to nonnectar birds, +0.207. The contrasts reversed or weakened for nearby-land distances; exploratory group-resampling ranges included zero. The >=50% diet subset did not retain a positive relative-to-bee slope. These results motivate separating diet classes and distance definitions, rather than claiming birds replace bees as isolation increases. Supporting analyses of bird wing shape and butterfly host-use breadth are retained in Supplementary Fig. S3: HWI–occupancy correlations were positive in nine of ten candidate archipelagos for all birds, but there was no robust nectar-specific advantage; butterfly documented larval host-family breadth declined in some source masks, while wingspan did not show a consistent filter. Source completeness prevents interpreting the latter as local host loss or superior establishment of specialists. [Evidence D]

### R4. Bird dietary classes differ in their association with recorded floral form (Fig. 4)
Across 32 tropical candidate islands with at least five historical Direct flower name units, coefficients for tubular form were +0.123372, −0.063260 and +0.046922 for the >=50%, partial and zero nectar-associated diet classes, respectively. Their specialist-minus-partial difference was +0.186632. All 11 estimable nested geography/coverage/state-set panels had positive tubular and extended-access contrasts; ten with estimable group omissions retained the positive tubular sign. In the public Direct min50 cohort of 24 islands, the tubular contrast was +0.258510; the family-restricted diet-label test had one-sided p=.0005, BH q=.006 across all 24 exploratory tests. Three tubular and three extended-access panels passed family-null BH; no genus-null comparison passed. In the same 24-island cohort, split-diet prediction reduced held-out within-group tubular-form MSE from .0075335 to .0022541 versus a broad nectar category, improving five of five groups. However, the broader 32-island cohort showed worse prediction, so the predictive gain was conditional. Red/pink × extended and nominal Q did not show consistent positive correspondence. These are dietary association results, not confirmed local floral preferences or functional replacement. [Evidence E]

### R5. Two-sided taxonomy separates raw correspondence from residual trait specificity (Fig. 5)
The original protected form-only data reproduced the positive specialist-minus-partial point estimates across all ten target and ten source-relative panels. Yet none of 500 plant genus-grade permutation tests across the larger original form/depth family survived BH. On exactly 30 common islands and the jointly scored plant roster, geo5 source-relative contrasts were +.019803 for tubular form, +.310323 for deep tube and +.169000 for tubular-and-deep. Exact genus-grade expectations were +.113496, +.114743 and +.204551, respectively; the resulting residuals were −.093692, +.195580 and −.035551. Climate10 produced residuals −.074534, +.187680 and −.030217. Thus morphology and depth need not show the same relationship with animal diet. The four positive unadjusted dual-null tests concerned depth in the same all-recorded 30-island cohort under alternative candidate sources/outcomes and bird-family constraints; 0/160 comparisons passed Holm, and no bird-genus IUT passed. In the 20-island common native-supported subset, the positive deep residual nearly disappeared before restricting the plant roster (−.009395 geo5; −.014964 climate10), and literal-native residuals were negative. In the preceding 30-island cohort median unresolved-origin share was .538995. All 460 feasible endpoint-wise partial-identification ranges included zero. Consequently we cannot identify a robust native floral–diet effect or interaction-mediated explanation of the tropical macroecological pattern. [Evidence F]

## Discussion

### Geographically contingent joint floral reassembly

A single directional shift in floral specialization is not sufficient to describe the observed island–candidate-source contrasts. More specifically, tropical floral reassembly varies with which architecture accompanies a deep tube: the radial combination increases while the bilateral combination has a negative point contrast, and their marginal-corrected covariance shifts are opposite. The tubular/bell combination illustrates why changes in joint-state abundance and changes in association cannot be substituted for one another. The geographically heterogeneous flower-size changes of Hetherington-Rauth and Johnson (2020) and the island occurrence/trait interactions of Zell et al. (2025) motivate moving beyond a single trait expectation. Here the focus is the joint symmetry–depth state: the tropical contrast is positive while the northern high-latitude contrast is negative. Symmetry and depth describe different aspects of flower architecture, and the radial/deep category is not inherently a measurement of generalism or specialization. Its excess is a geographically contingent assemblage pattern, not a demonstration that flowers have become more complex or specialized.

We distinguish the increase in joint frequency (+10.989 percentage points) from the excess covariance (+4.01790 points) on the focal tropical scored cohort. The first includes the product-of-marginals difference; the second isolates the association component. The covariance accounting associates nearly all of that difference with the between-genus term (+4.05652 points, within-genus −0.03862). The remaining +6.97068 points of the original joint-frequency contrast are the exact arithmetic difference in products of the marginals, not a separately fitted biological pathway. The opposite-sign zygomorphic/deep covariance is likewise predominantly between genera. This is evidence that the composition of documented floral architectures varies, not proof that the direction of selective specialization has changed. This provides a quantitative counterpart for floral traits to broader evidence that community assembly can restructure trait associations (Song et al., 2026). It complements island trait-space studies such as Barajas Barbosa et al. (2023) without estimating the historical processes they address. The genus terms depend on present-day documented species composition and within-genus trait frequencies: they are not causal effects of establishment or species turnover, and do not test within-species adaptation. The recurring category frequencies of species- and genus-disjoint floras likewise do not demonstrate excess convergence against a taxonomy-preserving null.

### What the independently compiled animal data add

Ishii et al. (2025) explicitly investigated plant-family contributions alongside pollinator fauna and floral characteristics. Our extension is to assess a particular joint floral pattern in a global candidate-source framework and to ask whether animal-associated signals persist when both assemblages' taxonomic composition is retained in conditional nulls. The local interaction evidence of Hiraiwa and Ushimaru (2024) is stronger for actual flower use and pollen transfer than the island-wide co-occurrence data available here. Our data broaden geographical comparisons but cannot replace these measurements.

Diet-class differentiation is a useful empirical lead rather than proof of an alternative pollination system. Specialist-minus-partial diet coefficients for the recorded tubular category were positive in eleven estimable nested public panels, and some exceeded family-conditioned bird diet-label expectations. This extends the *comparison* to independently compiled island bird and plant assemblages; it does not discover the distinction between specialist and generalist bird pollination, already discussed by Abrahamczyk (2019). In addition, the Diet.Nect fraction includes nectar, pollen and exudates: a partial diet is not equivalent to a demonstrated generalist pollinator. None of the stronger genus-conditioned and original two-sided tests establishes robust functional correspondence. In the common island cohort the tubular coefficient was smaller than the plant genus-grade expectation, while the deep-class residual was positive under both source definitions. The latter disappeared on restricting the islands to those with sufficient literal native records, even before changing the plant roster. The correct interpretation is a contrast between observational association and conditional evidence for specificity, not an animal-mediated causal explanation of tropical trait covariance.

### A working hypothesis linking assembly and possible interactions

We use *combinatorial floral reassembly* descriptively for changes in the frequency and covariance of documented floral states relative to a specified comparator. It is not a newly established syndrome. The mechanistic hypothesis is that geographical filters on both plant and animal assemblages alter the available combinations of potential partners, while resource-access traits may further constrain realized interactions. This distinguishes flight-associated occupancy, establishment resource requirements (including larval hosts), and potential flower use. The distance-sensitive bird-richness contrasts and documented butterfly host-breadth associations are exploratory motivation, not support for an identified sequence of colonization, establishment and mutualism.

A prospective test of architecture-dependent reassembly should first specify a candidate-source-relative floral combination and the expected local animal trait distribution independently of the observed floral effect. In particular, compare the distribution of symmetry categories conditional on a measured depth class, retain the full symmetry/depth contingency table rather than only positive cells, and assess whether the relative representation of partner access modes predicts the residual association under both plant and animal taxonomic nulls. The observed marginal and covariance contrasts motivate this test; they do not yet identify the conditional probability of radial symmetry among deep flowers or an animal-mediated shift. Use locally measured approach geometry, visitor mouthpart/bill reach and effective pollen transfer rather than assuming radial/deep is a bird/butterfly syndrome. It should then distinguish (i) a shift in marginal floral frequencies, (ii) a shift in floral covariance or lineage composition and (iii) residual floral–animal correspondence beyond fixed-incidence taxonomy-conditioned nulls. The latter should be tested on independent, geographically qualified groups with reliable native provenance and direct local visitor morphology, visitation and pollen transfer. A null result at the final stage would not negate the descriptive plant assemblage pattern; it would constrain a particular proposed interaction explanation.

### Limitations and inference ceilings

The animal and plant analyses cover different, nested sets of islands and have unequal geographical support; actual animal absence, abundance, pollen transport and local trait evolution are not observed. Native status is incompletely annotated, and recorded traits may reflect nonrandom documentation. Taxonomy-conditioned expectations are statistical decompositions, not phylogenetic mediation. Candidate source comparisons do not reconstruct mainland ancestors. The global tropical botanical pattern persists in the original literal-known-native subset, but cannot be generalized to the entire native flora without addressing nonrandom status and phenotype documentation. The much smaller native-supported animal-matched cohort, different endpoints and null tests do not establish a native floral–diet association. Do not transfer support for botanical contrasts to animal mechanism tests. The two-sided null family and unresolved-origin bounds explicitly delimit inference; no robust native sign or independent mechanism is identified. The global plant contrasts and geographically restricted animal tests address related but different estimands. The following predictions remain open: (i) diet composition improves prediction of floral access traits on independent archipelagos; (ii) matching persists under independent taxon and origin support; (iii) measured visitation and pollen deposition depend on resource-access matching conditional on visitor availability. These should be tested with independent data, not additional searches for favourable subsets.

### Conclusion

Documented island floral trait combinations show geographically and architecturally contingent differences relative to candidate mainland pools, rather than a common directional shift of all deep-tube combinations. Much of the tropical covariance excess is associated with genus composition. Independently compiled animal data yield exploratory dietary contrasts, but the conditional analyses prevent attributing the global botanical pattern to demonstrated pollinator replacement. The contribution is a joint-distribution account of floral assemblage differences and an explicit two-sided diagnostic of a proposed interaction mechanism. This provides a testable connection between functional island biogeography and pollination ecology while preserving the distinction between potential partner composition and realized mutualistic function.
