# Cover letter draft — Ecology Letters first shot

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Letter, **“Island isolation is associated with recurrent reproductive assurance but regionally contingent floral change,”** for publication in *Ecology Letters*.

A long-standing expectation in island biology is that isolation should favour reproductive independence when compatible mates or effective pollen delivery become unreliable. Yet that expectation is often translated into a single floral island syndrome, even though the same reproductive problem need not produce the same flower phenotype in every region. We ask a more general question: **which reproductive functions recur with island isolation, which floral traits remain geographically contingent, and does the same isolation gradient carry an independently measured reproductive constraint?**

We combine two independent global evidence layers. The island-flora dataset spans 106,295 angiosperm species and a corrected universe of 8,264 island units. Rather than average traits into a composite syndrome score, we estimate seven reproductive, colour and structural traits separately in four geographic regions. Self-compatibility increases with isolation in all four regions in both the broad contemporary flora and the WCVP regional-native-compatible sensitivity. Other reproductive and floral responses are less uniform: selfing, generalized form, actinomorphy, tube depth and colour differ among regions. Conditional analyses further show that generalized floral accessibility is not statistically exhausted by measured reproductive assurance, with FDR-supported selfing-adjusted responses in northern high latitudes and the tropical all-analysis.

A post-baseline source-matched diagnostic further asks where the compositional response resides. Where support is adequate, the northern-midlatitude response is concentrated in genus composition rather than additional species sorting within represented genera; in the tropics, source-pool and realized-genus components move in opposite directions. The second independent evidence layer consists of 2,969 pollen-supplementation experiments. Experimental pollen limitation increases with geographic isolation (β = 0.0919, finite-publication P = 0.01594), and the positive offshore-only gradient remains after continental zero-distance sites are excluded. In exact-species post-hoc comparisons, the reproductive-assurance and generalized-accessibility states enriched along the island gradient are each associated with lower current pollen limitation (P = 0.00417 and 0.02334).

The conceptual advance is therefore **functional recurrence without phenotypic uniformity**. Island isolation is associated most consistently with reproductive-assurance traits, while the visible floral phenotype through which insularity is expressed remains region dependent. The same geographic axis is independently associated with stronger pollen limitation, and the island-associated response states are functionally aligned with lower contemporary limitation. This constraint–response triangle links island macroecology to experimental plant reproduction without requiring a universal flower type or a named global pollinator mechanism.

We explicitly limit the inference. The study does not establish historical mediation from pollen limitation to contemporary trait composition, a global decline in pollinator abundance or visitation, or within-species evolution. The source-matched diagnostic localizes a compositional response but does not identify colonization, establishment, persistence or extinction as its demographic cause. H2 is conditional decomposition and H4 is post-hoc functional triangulation. The corrected geographic exposure is a post-hoc measurement repair, not a new prospective confirmation, and the H1 individual tests are pointwise rather than a family-wise declaration of a universal syndrome.

We believe this fits *Ecology Letters* because it reframes a classic island-syndrome question around a general ecological distinction: repeated environmental constraints can generate recurrent functions without uniform phenotypes. The study combines global island biogeography, trait ecology and independent experimental evidence to identify that distinction at broad taxonomic and geographic scales.
